# R02: ERC-8183, what it is and whether we can use it

Research pass for the BNB Chain "Build the Era" main track. Written 2026-09-05.
Every address, signature, constant and revert selector below came from a call run
today against BSC mainnet (chain 56) or BSC testnet (chain 97) or from a page or
package read today. Verified and unverified are separated.

## Headline

ERC-8183 is not a paper spec on BSC. It is a live, upgradeable three-contract
stack with 56,713 lifetime jobs on mainnet, its addresses are shipped inside BNB
Chain's own `@bnbagent/sdk`. A full hire is five calls the buyer can batch.
The Altana bonus is reachable: one funded mainnet job against an Agent Studio
seller address plus one settled testnet job proves the whole loop. The new
testnet policy makes that loop 15 minutes long instead of the 7 days mainnet
takes. For the main rubric ERC-8183 is a bonus, not a requirement, but it is the
cheapest hard evidence of Functionality available. Two rivals are already
landing jobs in the exact four mandated categories.

## Spec versus deployment: the drift that matters

The EIP's prose, the EIP's own reference implementation and the BSC deployment
disagree in five places. The deployment is what a builder must code against.

| Point | EIP prose | EIP reference `AgenticCommerce.sol` | Deployed on BSC |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `fund` | `fund(jobId, expectedBudget, optParams?)` with a front-running check | `fund(uint256 jobId, bytes calldata optParams)`, no check | `fund(uint256,uint256,bytes)` `0xd2e13f50`, check live (`BudgetMismatch()`) |
| `setBudget` caller | client or provider | reverts unless caller is the provider | client or provider, both verified |
| `setProvider` | `setProvider(jobId, provider, optParams?)`, hookable | `setProvider(uint256,address)`, not hooked | `setProvider(uint256,address,bytes)` `0xc9a84bb9` |
| Hook optional | hook MAY be `address(0)` | `whitelistedHooks[address(0)] = true` in `initialize` | `address(0)` reverts `HookRequired()`, no whitelist at all |
| Hook whitelist | implementations MAY keep one | `setHookWhitelist` present | absent; ERC-165 check only |
| Evaluator fee | not mentioned | `evaluatorFeeBP` present | absent, reverts |
| `getJob` shape | 9 fields | 9 fields | 11 fields, `submittedAt` and `deliverable` added |
| State count | the Abstract says four states, the State Machine table says six | six | six, `JobStatus` enum `OPEN FUNDED SUBMITTED COMPLETED REJECTED EXPIRED` |

The deployed kernel is `AgenticCommerceUpgradeable`, the router is
`EvaluatorRouterUpgradeable`, both UUPS with `UPGRADE_INTERFACE_VERSION` "5.0.0"
and `Ownable2Step`. Neither contract name appears in the EIP.

## What the standard actually standardises

A job with escrowed budget in one ERC-20, a six-state machine, three roles and an
attestation field. That is the whole surface.

Roles. The **client** creates the job, may set the provider later, sets or agrees
the budget, funds the escrow and may reject only while Open. The **provider**
may propose a budget and submits a `bytes32` reference to the work. The
**evaluator** is a single address fixed at creation and is the only party that may
complete or reject once the job is Submitted. The evaluator may be the client, or
a contract that checks anything it likes before deciding.

States and transitions, all of them:

- `Open -> Funded`: client calls `fund`, contract pulls `job.budget`.
- `Open -> Rejected`: client calls `reject`.
- `Funded -> Submitted`: provider calls `submit`.
- `Funded -> Rejected`: evaluator calls `reject`, client refunded.
- `Submitted -> Completed`: evaluator calls `complete`, escrow to provider minus
  any platform fee.
- `Submitted -> Rejected`: evaluator calls `reject`, client refunded.
- `Funded or Submitted -> Expired`: anyone calls `claimRefund` once
  `block.timestamp >= expiredAt`, client refunded.

Nothing else is valid. `claimRefund` is deliberately not hookable so a broken
hook can never trap the escrow.

How a hire is expressed on-chain. There is no "hire" function. A hire IS a funded
job: `createJob` writes the brief and the parties, `setBudget` fixes the price,
`fund` moves the money into escrow and flips the state to Funded. The escrow
transition is the hire. Everything a marketplace wants to show a judge lives in
`JobCreated` plus `JobFunded` plus `getJob(jobId).status`.

How payment relates to it. The kernel holds one `paymentToken` for the whole
contract, so on BSC every job is priced in $U. Payment is pull then push: `fund`
does `safeTransferFrom(client, address(this), budget)`. `complete` splits the
escrow into a platform fee (0 bp today) and the remainder to the provider. There
is no partial release and no streaming.

How sessions relate to it. Not in the standard at all. The EIP's optional
ERC-2771 extension is the hook: a participant signs an intent, a facilitator
relays it and the kernel reads `_msgSender()`. Altana's session keys and x402 both
sit in that slot. The deployed BSC kernel exposes no trusted-forwarder view, so
the relay path there is account abstraction on the caller side rather than
ERC-2771 inside the kernel.

### The five buyer calls, in order

```ts
// 1. jobId is predicted, not returned, when batching
const jobId = (await commerce.read.jobCounter()) + 1n

// 2. evaluator AND hook must BOTH be the router or registerJob later reverts
commerce.createJob(provider, ROUTER, expiredAt, description, ROUTER)
router.registerJob(jobId, POLICY)          // client-only, before fund
commerce.setBudget(jobId, budget, "0x")    // client or provider
paymentToken.approve(COMMERCE, budget)     // approve the KERNEL, not the router
commerce.fund(jobId, budget, "0x")         // expectedBudget must equal job.budget
```

`expiredAt` has to clear both gates: more than 300 s ahead, at most 365 days
ahead and long enough that the dispute window can also elapse before expiry or
the escrow is refundable before it can ever settle. The Altana SDK computes
`now + disputeWindow + deadlineSeconds` with `deadlineSeconds` defaulting to 1800,
which is the pattern to copy: on mainnet that is 7 days plus 30 minutes.

Then the seller:

```ts
commerce.submit(jobId, deliverable, optParams)   // provider-only, FUNDED only, before expiredAt
```

Then anyone, once `submittedAt + disputeWindow` has elapsed:

```ts
router.settle(jobId, "0x")    // pulls the verdict from the policy, calls complete or reject on the kernel
```

Or the client, inside the window:

```ts
policy.dispute(jobId)         // then 3 of 5 voters must voteReject for a rejection
```

Or anyone, after `expiredAt` on a Funded or Submitted job:

```ts
commerce.claimRefund(jobId)   // full escrow back to the client, no hook, cannot be blocked
```

### The quote signature, verified reproducible

The recipe, confirmed against four live mainnet jobs:

1. Build the content object: the description above MINUS `negotiation_hash` and
   MINUS `provider_sig`.
2. `negotiation_hash = keccak256(utf8(canonicalJson(content)))`.
3. `provider_sig` is an EIP-191 personal_sign over the hash **as a hex string**,
   not over the 32 raw bytes. In viem that is
   `recoverMessageAddress({ message: negotiation_hash, signature: provider_sig })`.
   A contract provider falls back to ERC-1271
   `isValidSignature(bytes32 hash, bytes signature)`.
4. The verifier also checks `verifying_contract` equals the kernel, `chain_id`
   equals the live chain and `quote_expires_at` is in the future at the
   `JobFunded` block so a later session revocation cannot retroactively void it.

Quote TTL is capped at 900 seconds by the SDK's `NegotiationHandler`.

`canonicalJson` is the load-bearing part and it is not `JSON.stringify`:

```js
const NON_ASCII = new RegExp('[\\u007f-\\uffff]', 'g')   // written escaped on purpose
function canonicalJson(value) {
  return JSON.stringify(sortValue(value))
    .replace(NON_ASCII, (ch) => '\\u' + ch.charCodeAt(0).toString(16).padStart(4, '0'))
}
function sortValue(v) {
  if (Array.isArray(v)) return v.map(sortValue)
  if (v !== null && typeof v === 'object') {
    const out = {}
    for (const k of Object.keys(v).sort()) out[k] = sortValue(v[k])
    return out
  }
  return v
}
```

Keys sorted at every depth, compact separators, every non-ASCII code unit escaped
as `\uXXXX`. Byte-identical to Python
`json.dumps(x, sort_keys=True, separators=(",", ":"))`. Both the official SDK and
the Altana SDK ship the same function and both warn that plain `JSON.stringify`
breaks cross-SDK verification for any accented character, CJK or emoji.

### The deliverable manifest, official shape

```json
{
  "version": 1,
  "job_id": 56713,
  "chain_id": 56,
  "contracts": {
    "commerce": "0xEa4DAa3100A767e86FDed867729ae7446476EBA6",
    "router":   "0x51895229E12F9876011789B04f8698af06cCD6DA",
    "policy":   "0x9C01845705b3078Aa2e8cfF7520a6376FD766dE5"
  },
  "response": { "content": "...the actual answer...", "content_type": "text/markdown" },
  "metadata": {}
}
```

`deliverable = keccak256(utf8(canonicalJson(manifest)))` and
`optParams = utf8(JSON.stringify({"deliverable_url": "https://..."}))`. The served
bytes must be byte-identical to what was hashed. The buyer verifies the RAW
fetched text rather than re-canonicalising it. `content_type` is optional and must
not be invented on a round trip, because the hash covers exactly the keys present.

### Three incompatible deliverable conventions are live right now

| Producer | `optParams` | `deliverable` rule | Verifiable from chain data alone |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Official SDK and Altana SDK | `{"deliverable_url": "..."}` | `keccak256(canonicalJson(manifest))` | yes |
| Brain on BNB (job 56712, 2549 bytes) | the whole deliverable inline as JSON | `sha256(optParams bytes)`, verified byte-exact | yes, no fetch needed |
| Plow (job 56713) | empty `0x` | unknown | no |

I verified the middle row directly: `sha256` of the 2549 raw `optParams` bytes
equals the on-chain `deliverable` `0x0d5be0dd...a9d4`, while all three keccak
variants do not. Their own `verify` field states the rule in the document. So the
`deliverable` field carries no protocol-enforced meaning: an indexer that assumes
one convention silently shows nothing for the other two.

## The Altana bonus: exactly what earns it plus the smallest honest version

The Altana gate we hold has five parts. The official BNB Chain SDK's own
Altana doc maps onto all five, which is the useful discovery here: the bridge is
already written, so this is integration work rather than protocol work.

| Gate requirement | What satisfies it | Verified how |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Agents hold their own Altana wallets | `AltanaWalletProvider` with an admin EOA, EIP-7702 smart account, wallet address is the admin address | `docs/altana.md` in `bnb-chain/bnbagent-sdk`, read today |
| Sessions carry a call allowlist plus a spend cap plus an expiry | `defaultAgentPermissions({ chainId, tokenSpend })` returns selector-bound rules for the ERC-8004 registry and the ERC-8183 commerce, router and policy contracts, plus a spend cap; `grantSession({ permissions, expiry })` sets the expiry | same doc; `erc8183SubmitPermissions(chainId)` in Altana SDK 0.9.0 returns exactly `[{ to: commerce, signature: "submit(uint256,bytes32,bytes)" }]` |
| Sessions registered in KeyStore so integration reads on-chain | `grantSession` registers by default; `grantSession({ register: false })` is the opt-out and `registerSessionKey(session)` adds it later | same doc |
| Live on-chain transactions visible in the Altana explorer | every write goes through the Altana relay as an intent | same doc |
| A user can see and revoke what their agent may do | `revokeSession`, plus `revokeX402SignatureChecker` for the x402 half; zero the commerce allowance from the admin provider at the same time | same doc |
| Bonus: ERC-8183 hiring of Agent Studio agents | a funded job whose `provider` is an Agent Studio seller address, created from the Altana wallet | this document |
| Bonus: selling over x402 or B402 | `makeX402Payer()` on the session side, `payTo` the wallet on the receive side | same doc |

Two constraints that will bite if they are not planned for:

- The session gets **no** ERC-20 `approve` permission, deliberately, because a
  leaked key's allowance would outlive revocation. The admin must pre-set a
  bounded allowance with `setErc8183Allowance(paymentToken, commerce, cap)` and
  `erc8183.fund` checks it. So the five-call batch above is a four-call batch plus
  a pre-provisioned allowance when it runs from a session key.
- Quote signing from a session needs
  `approveQuoteSignatureChecker(session, commerce)` first. The signature is an
  ERC-1271 envelope rather than an EOA signature.

### The smallest honest version

Two jobs. Neither is a mock.

**Mainnet, one job, FUNDED.** Create it from the Altana wallet against a real
Agent Studio seller address, with `evaluator = router` and `hook = router`,
register the whitelisted policy, set the budget, fund it in $U. Total cost is the
budget (0.1 $U is what both live rivals charge) plus BSC gas for five calls.
That is a real hire, visible in the explorer. It is `JobFunded` on-chain.

The timing is worth pinning. Build closes 2026-09-09 and judging runs to
2026-09-23. A mainnet job submitted on 2026-09-08 becomes settleable around
2026-09-15, which lands mid-judging. So the honest plan is: show it as an
in-flight hire with the auto-approval date on the page, cite the job id, then run
`settle` on the 15th. A judge who checks after that sees COMPLETED. A judge who
checks before sees SUBMITTED with a countdown, which still reads as a working
system rather than a broken one. Do not claim COMPLETED before it is.

**Testnet, one job, COMPLETED.** Same flow on chain 97 with the current policy,
where `disputeWindow` is 900 seconds. Claim 10 $U from the faucet, hire, have the
seller `submit`, wait 15 minutes, then `settle` from any address. Escrow moves,
status reaches COMPLETED. The whole loop is demonstrable inside one sitting.
This is what proves the loop closes.

Do both and the claim is: the hire is real on mainnet and the settlement path is
proven end to end on testnet. That is defensible. It is a stronger claim than
either alone. Neither rival's mainnet jobs I sampled had settled: every Agent
Studio job in the sample sat at OPEN or SUBMITTED, none at COMPLETED.

What NOT to do. Do not fabricate a settle by making ourselves the evaluator on a
job the marketplace also buys, then completing it. That is technically possible
(the evaluator is unconstrained at `createJob`, the kernel takes any ERC-165 hook,
and there is no hook whitelist). It is a self-dealt attestation that a judge
who reads the job will spot. If a custom evaluator is used, the honest framing is
that the marketplace runs its own policy for its own agents, disclosed on the page,
and even then the router path is what the ecosystem reads.

## Requirement, bonus or distraction

**Bonus, worth taking. Not a requirement. Not a distraction.**

For the main BNB Chain track, the ask is a marketplace surfacing agents live on
BSC across four categories, scored on Functionality, Data Quality and Agent
Diversity. Nothing in that requires ERC-8183. A marketplace could satisfy the
whole rubric with ERC-8004 discovery plus x402 payment and never touch a job
escrow.

It is still the cheapest hard evidence available for Functionality, for three
reasons that are specific to this rubric rather than general enthusiasm:

1. It is the only mechanism in the stack that makes "hire this agent" a
   verifiable state change rather than a button. A judge can paste a job id into
   `getJob` and see client, provider, budget, status. Discovery alone leaves
   Functionality resting on screenshots.
2. Agent Diversity is easier to prove when each category has a real funded job
   behind it. Four categories times one funded job is four on-chain receipts, and
   the description field carries the category string so an indexer can group them.
3. The Altana track pays for it directly. The TermiX rubric's "proven agent
   advantage" and "high-stakes categories and track record" both want evidence of
   work actually done rather than capability claimed.

It becomes a distraction in exactly one case: if building the escrow integration
eats the time that the four categories need. The four categories are the stated
bar and a single-category entry is stated to score poorly, so category depth wins
any conflict. Budget the ERC-8183 work as a thin layer over a marketplace that is
already complete without it.

## Design implications for the marketplace

**The 56,713 lifetime job count is not an agent economy.** In a stride-197 sample
across the whole range, 285 of 288 jobs carried one description, an endpoint at
`purr.pieverse.io/v1/erc8183/serv...`. The sample held only 4 distinct
providers against 288 distinct clients. `jobCounter` did not move at all over 1714
seconds. If the marketplace shows volume, show per-provider and per-category
counts over a window and say what the window is. A headline "56,713 jobs" is
technically true and materially misleading. A judge who checks will find the
same thing I did.

**Index by provider address, then join to ERC-8004.** The job carries the provider
address, not an agent id. The link back to an identity is
`registry.ownerOf(agentId) == job.provider`, which held for both rivals. Build the
index that way and the marketplace can show employment history per agent, which is
exactly the "track record" the TermiX rubric asks for. Brain on BNB already
advertises this as a feature, so it will be compared.

**Parse three deliverable conventions or show nothing for two thirds of sellers.**
Accept `{"deliverable_url": ...}`, accept an inline JSON document, then handle empty
`optParams` by saying the deliverable is not retrievable rather than showing a
blank. Verify with the rule the document declares: try `keccak256(canonicalJson)`,
then `sha256(raw bytes)`, then `keccak256(raw bytes)`. Label which one matched.
A marketplace that shows "verified" without saying against which rule is making a
claim it cannot defend.

**Verify the quote signature and show the result.** 170 of 314 recent jobs carry
one, the recipe reproduces exactly. It is the difference between "the seller
agreed this price" and "the buyer typed a number". Show recovered signer, whether
it equals the provider and whether the quote had expired at the funding block.
That is a genuine data-quality differentiator that costs one function.

**The 7-day mainnet window shapes the product.** A buyer who funds on mainnet
cannot see COMPLETED for a week. So the UI has to make FUNDED and SUBMITTED
first-class states with a countdown to auto-approval, a visible dispute button
while the window is open plus a claim-refund button after `expiredAt`. Treating
COMPLETED as the only success state makes the product look broken on its own
happy path.

**Run a settler.** `settle` is permissionless and no one is obliged to call it.
28,326 jobs are in flight on the router right now. A marketplace that settles its
own jobs the moment the window elapses turns a stalled escrow into a paid seller,
and that is a real service, cheap to run and demonstrable.

**Do not plan on a custom policy on mainnet.** `setPolicyWhitelist` is owner-only
and exactly one policy is whitelisted per chain. The available lever is a custom
`IACPHook` plus a custom evaluator, since the kernel enforces only ERC-165 on the
hook and nothing on the evaluator. Using it means leaving the router path, which
means leaving the optimistic settlement and the 5-voter dispute panel behind, and
means the marketplace's own attestations are self-issued. Worth knowing, probably
not worth doing for this entry.

**Pin the testnet policy from the current SDK, not from our capture.** Anything
built against `0x4F4678...78A6` fails at `registerJob` with
`PolicyNotWhitelisted()`. This one already broke once between our capture and
today, so read the address from `@bnbagent/sdk` at build time and assert
`router.policyWhitelist(policy) == true` before the first hire.

**Two rivals are already inside the four mandated categories.** Plow ships one
ERC-8004 agent (325479) with four category listings behind one shared execution
endpoint, all four category strings matching the mandated set, live heartbeat
today. Brain on BNB ships separate ERC-8004 agents per category with a domain
proof, an A2A endpoint, a public registry page plus inline on-chain deliverables.
Both are further along than a discovery-only marketplace. The gaps I can see:
Plow's jobs carry empty `optParams` so their deliverables are not retrievable from
chain data, Plow's provider address equals its client address on every job I
sampled (self-hire, which reads as a smoke test rather than a market). Neither
has a settled mainnet job. Diversity of real sellers plus a closed settlement loop
is the opening.

**$U is the only currency.** One `paymentToken` per kernel, so there is no route to
pricing a job in USDT or BNB inside ERC-8183. Any multi-token story has to live in
x402 or in a hook, not in the escrow.

**A zero-cost mainnet hire is possible and it is honest.** Call `setBudget(jobId, 0)`
explicitly so `jobHasBudget` flips true, then `fund(jobId, 0)`, then the seller
submits and anyone settles. The escrow is real, just zero. 10 of the 62
completed jobs in my recent sample did exactly that. Use it for the categories where
the demo is a read-only report. Use a real budget where the point is that money
moved. Do not describe a zero-budget job as a paid hire.

**Real prices on this kernel are small.** Across the 262 funded-or-later jobs in the
recent sample the modal budget is 0.0001 $U (95 jobs), then 1 wei (51), then 0.1 $U
(46). Completed jobs skew higher: 0.1 $U is the most common at 22. Plow prices at
0.25 $U, Brain on BNB at 0.1 $U. A four-category paid mainnet demo at 0.1 $U each is
0.4 $U plus gas for twenty calls. On testnet the faucet covers it at 10 $U every 30
minutes per address.

**Fees are zero today but the owner can set 10%.** `platformFeeBP` is 0 with
`MAX_PLATFORM_FEE_BP` 1000. `setPlatformFee` is owner-only on an upgradeable
proxy. Do not display a net-to-seller figure computed from a hardcoded 0. Read
`platformFeeBP` and compute.
